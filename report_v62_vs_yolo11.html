01 What the model does

It finds and outlines every component pad on a printed circuit board, one mask per physical object, and labels each with its shape class. Not a bounding box around a pad — the actual pixel boundary of that pad, separated from the pad touching it.

Four classes, taken from the annotation set: Rectangle, Rectangle_concave, circle (an annulus) and circle_full (a filled disc). Input is letterboxed to 512 × 512; instance reasoning happens on a 256 × 256 grid and the class map is produced at full resolution.

Two properties of this dataset shape every design decision that follows. The objects are small — a median circle_full is 16 pixels across in the test split, so a one-pixel error around its rim costs roughly a fifth of its IoU. And the test split is not the training split: its objects are 0.48–0.76× the linear size of training objects and sit 2.4× more densely per image. A model that only ever saw training-scale objects would meet something different at inference.

02 How it works

A shared encoder feeds two independent decoders, which feed four supervised heads. There are no bounding boxes anywhere in the design; an instance is a set of pixels that agree on where their centre is.

Semantic head

512 × 512 × 5

Per-pixel class. Trained with focal + Dice + Lovász-Softmax, the last optimising IoU directly rather than as a proxy.

Centre head

256 × 256 × 4

One Gaussian peak per object, per class. CenterNet focal loss. Peaks after non-maximum suppression become instance seeds.

Offset head

256 × 256 × 2

Each foreground pixel predicts the vector to its own object's centre. Pixels are then assigned to the seed they point at.

Boundary head

512 × 512 × 1

Supervised on instance-ID transitions. Splits candidates that the centre grouping merged, and sharpens the class map's edges.

The encoder borrows YOLO's C3k2/SPPF blocks, the neck is a weighted bidirectional feature pyramid, and the instance grouping is the centre + offset scheme from Panoptic-DeepLab. 6,417,683 parameters across 464 layers, 42.75 GMAC per image.

Augmentation matched to the measured domain gap

Because the test split is smaller and denser than the training split, the training pipeline manufactures that shift: a 95-mode rotation and photometric cycle, all eight dihedral transforms (every class here is symmetric under them, so they are exactly label-preserving), a zoom-out ladder from 5% to 50% in 5% steps padded with black, and copy-paste augmentation that lifts Rectangle_concave from 0.47% of objects to roughly a quarter of the objects the model sees.

04 Why the gap sits at high IoU

Three structural differences explain it, and all three are properties of the architectures rather than of how long either was trained.

Prototype masks are low-rank; dense masks are not

YOLO-seg predicts a small basis of prototype masks at reduced resolution and reconstructs each instance as a weighted combination of them. That is efficient and works well on large, distinct objects. Every mask it can express, though, lives in that low-dimensional span. On a 16-pixel disc, the rim is most of the object, and the rim is what the basis cannot spend capacity on. V6.2 predicts a class per pixel at full 512 px resolution; nothing constrains one mask to resemble a blend of others.

A mask cropped to a box inherits the box's error

YOLO-seg multiplies its mask by the detected bounding box. A box that is slightly loose admits background; slightly tight, and it shaves the object. At IoU 0.5 that rarely changes the verdict. At IoU 0.85 it decides it. V6.2 produces no boxes at all — an instance is a connected set of pixels whose predicted offsets agree on a centre — so there is no box error to inherit.

Axis-aligned boxes are a poor description of a rotated pad

These pads sit at arbitrary angles; the training cycle alone sweeps 45 distinct rotations. An axis-aligned box around a diagonal elongated pad is mostly background, which weakens both the mask crop and the non-maximum suppression that decides which detections survive. Dense per-pixel grouping has no orientation preference to lose.

A fourth difference is supervision rather than structure: V6.2 has an explicit boundary head trained on instance-ID transitions. Nothing in YOLO-seg is supervised on where one object stops and the next begins, and at 77 objects per test image, plenty of them touch.

05 What this does not yet prove

The comparison above is indicative, not decisive. Five things stand between it and a claim that would survive review.

  • Unverified
    The two models may not share a validation split. The YOLO run referenced a Split_Data_yolo directory that no longer exists. Until both are scored on the same images, the difference includes an unknown amount of split effect.
  • Unverified
    Two different mAP implementations. Ultralytics computes its own; V6.2 computes its own. Both follow the COCO convention, but they have not been checked against each other on identical inputs.
  • Open
    Neither model is converged. YOLO stopped at 23 of a planned 300 epochs; V6.2 is at 23 of 120. Both numbers will move, and not necessarily by the same amount.
  • Open
    No error bars. With 432 test images, the difference between 0.78 and 0.80 may well be inside the sampling noise. A 1,000-fold bootstrap is needed; overlapping intervals would mean nothing has been beaten.
  • Known
    The labels cap what any model can score. 434 training images and 19.4% of the test split contain polygons that overwrite one another — 104 training instances erased outright. That is a ceiling on achievable IoU that no architecture removes.

One further asymmetry is worth stating plainly rather than burying: YOLO arrived COCO-pretrained, with a large visual prior. V6.2 started from random weights and 4,680 images. That makes the current gap more surprising, not less — but it also means V6.2 carries no transferable prior to a different board type, where YOLO would.
